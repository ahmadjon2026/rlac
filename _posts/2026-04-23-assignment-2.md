## Introduction
Science fiction is often treated as a genre of imagination, but it is also a genre built on repetition. Across stories about alien planets, machines, war, and future civilizations, writers frequently return to similar themes, vocabulary, and narrative structures. This makes science fiction an interesting genre for computational analysis because it raises a difficult question: when computers read these texts, do they recognize individual authors, or do they mostly recognize the larger genre patterns connecting them together? This project compares two computational methods, Stylo and TF-IDF, using a corpus of eighteen science fiction texts from Project Gutenberg written between the late nineteenth century and the early 1960s. The corpus includes works by Philip K. Dick, Andre Norton, Leigh Brackett, Henry Kuttner, H.G. Wells, and Marion Zimmer Bradley. Some texts are planetary adventures, others focus on Cold War paranoia and machines, while one is a nonfiction political essay. The variety of the corpus makes it a strong test case because the methods must navigate differences in authorship, genre, tone, and narrative form at the same time.

The two methods approach texts differently. Stylo focuses on the most frequent words and is often used to detect writing style and authorship, while TF-IDF focuses on distinctive vocabulary and is more sensitive to topic and thematic content. At first, the methods appear to divide cleanly between style and content, but the results of this project suggest that the relationship is more complicated. Some authors, especially Philip K. Dick and Andre Norton, remain relatively stable across both methods, while several texts repeatedly emerge as outliers regardless of method, particularly H.G. Wells’s *The Salvaging of Civilization* and Marion Zimmer Bradley’s *Jackie Sees a Star.* At the same time, as the number of most frequent words increases, many texts begin moving closer together rather than farther apart, revealing the influence of a shared science fiction vocabulary shaped by mid-century pulp conventions, Cold War anxieties, and planetary exploration. Following Ted Underwood’s warning about the risks of distant reading, this essay treats computational outputs as starting points for interpretation rather than objective conclusions. By comparing Stylo and TF-IDF side by side, this project argues that computational reading reveals not only individual authorial habits, but also the larger genre pressures that shape speculative fiction.

## 2. The Corpus
The corpus consists of eighteen science fiction texts drawn from Project Gutenberg and written between 1896 and 1961. Although all the texts belong broadly to speculative fiction, they represent very different literary moments and subgenres. H.G. Wells’s works come from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and reflect an earlier phase of science fiction focused on invasion, evolution, and political thought. By contrast, many of the mid-century texts were written during or after World War II, at a time when science fiction became increasingly shaped by Cold War fears, rapid technological change, and pulp magazine culture. Philip K. Dick’s stories repeatedly explore paranoia, machines, and unstable realities, while Andre Norton’s works focus more heavily on planetary exploration and survival. Leigh Brackett combines science fiction with adventure and planetary romance, Henry Kuttner moves across comedy, horror, and action, and Marion Zimmer Bradley’s texts range from large-scale planetary settings to the much smaller and more intimate Jackie Sees a Star.

This variety makes the corpus analytically useful because the texts differ not only in authorship, but also in tone, genre, narrative perspective, and literary purpose. Some works are action-heavy pulp adventures, others are philosophical or political, and one text, H.G. Wells’s *The Salvaging of Civilization,* is nonfiction rather than narrative fiction. The corpus also includes a co-authored text, *The Black Kiss,* written by Henry Kuttner and Robert Bloch, which complicates the idea of a single stable authorial voice. These differences matter because the computational methods are not simply sorting isolated texts. They are attempting to classify works that often share the same broader science fiction environment while still maintaining individual stylistic habits. This tension between shared genre conventions and individual authorial identity becomes one of the central patterns revealed throughout the analysis.

## 3. Methods
This project uses two computational methods, Stylo and TF-IDF, to compare patterns of similarity across the corpus. Although both methods analyze word frequencies, they focus on different aspects of the texts and therefore produce different kinds of relationships between them. The Stylo package in R was used to examine stylistic similarity through the most frequent words (MFWs) in the corpus. These are usually common function words such as **“the,” “and,” or “of,”** which are less connected to topic and more connected to unconscious writing habits. Several forms of analysis were used, including cluster analysis, Bootstrap Consensus Trees (BCT), and Principal Components Analysis (PCA). Different MFW settings were tested in order to compare how stable the clusters remained across changing parameters.

TF-IDF was used to examine thematic and content-based similarity. Unlike Stylo, TF-IDF emphasizes words that are distinctive within particular texts rather than words that appear frequently across the whole corpus. PCA visualizations at different MFW settings were used to compare how texts grouped according to shared vocabulary and themes. Rather than treating the visualizations as objective conclusions, this project follows Ted Underwood’s argument that distant reading requires interpretation. The computational outputs are therefore used as evidence for literary analysis rather than as final answers by themselves.

## 4. Analysis
The computational analysis reveals both stable patterns and important contradictions across the corpus. Some authors remain highly recognizable across multiple methods and parameter settings, while other texts repeatedly resist classification or shift depending on the method being used. The **first part** of the analysis examines how Stylo identifies patterns of authorial stability through clustering and PCA visualizations. The **second part** focuses on stylistic outliers and the role of narrative form in shaping computational distance. The **third part** turns to TF-IDF in order to examine how thematic vocabulary reorganizes the corpus around shared science fiction concerns and genre language. **Finally,** the analysis compares where the two methods agree and where they begin to diverge, revealing that style, content, and genre are often more interconnected than the methods initially suggest.

## 4.1 Stylo and Authorial Stability
The first part of the analysis examines whether Stylo can consistently identify authors through patterns in the most frequent words. Since Stylo mainly relies on common grammatical words rather than thematic vocabulary, it is designed to detect writing habits rather than topic. The goal here is to see which authors maintain stable stylistic signatures across the corpus and which texts begin to break those patterns.

## 4.1.1 Reflection
Together, these two figures show that stylometry works best when an author maintains a relatively stable narrative style across texts. Dick and Norton fit this pattern clearly. However, the results also complicate the idea that every author has one fixed stylistic fingerprint. Writers like Kuttner and Brackett move across genres, tones, and narrative structures, which weakens stable clustering. The repeated isolation of *The Salvaging of Civilization* and *Jackie Sees a Star* becomes especially important because both texts differ from the corpus not simply in topic, but in form and voice. This suggests that style cannot always be separated cleanly from genre or content. In several cases, the computational method may be detecting broader literary differences rather than pure authorship alone.

Instead of showing direct clusters, PCA reduces complex stylistic variation into two main dimensions, making it easier to identify which texts remain close together and which ones become isolated from the rest of the corpus.

## 4.2.1 Reflection
This figure shows that stylistic similarity in the corpus is shaped not only by authorship, but also by shared genre conventions. Many of the texts compress toward the middle because they rely on similar narrative structures, vocabulary, and rhythms common to science fiction of the period. The most revealing cases are therefore the outliers rather than the central cluster. *The Salvaging of Civilization* and *Jackie Sees a Star* repeatedly resist integration into the larger stylistic field, suggesting that shifts in genre, narrative voice, and intended audience can reshape computational distance more strongly than authorship itself. The PCA therefore complicates the idea that stylometry simply identifies authors. In several cases, it appears to map broader literary form and narrative function instead.

## 4.3 TF-IDF and Thematic Clustering
Unlike Stylo, which focuses on writing habits and frequent grammatical words, TF-IDF highlights vocabulary that is distinctive to particular texts. This shifts the analysis away from authorship and toward thematic similarity. The PCA visualizations below therefore map the corpus according to what the texts are mainly about rather than how the authors write.

The strongest outlier is again *Jackie Sees a Star,* which separates sharply from the rest of the corpus. Unlike the earlier stylistic analyses, TF-IDF makes this distance even more visible because the story’s vocabulary differs strongly from the dominant language of space warfare, alien worlds, and planetary adventure found across much of the corpus. The visualization therefore shows thematic separation more aggressively than Stylo.

## 4.3.1 Reflection
Together, these two figures show that TF-IDF organizes the corpus primarily around thematic vocabulary rather than authorial style. At lower word counts, texts remain more visibly separated because distinctive vocabulary dominates the analysis. At higher word counts, many of the texts begin collapsing toward a shared science fiction center shaped by recurring genre language. However, the persistence of outliers such as *Jackie Sees a Star* and Wells’s nonfiction writing suggests that some thematic differences remain too strong to disappear even within a highly compressed genre space. Compared to Stylo, TF-IDF appears much more sensitive to topic, narrative setting, and recurring genre vocabulary than to stable authorial fingerprints.

## 4.4 Where the Methods Agree and Disagree
The comparison between Stylo and TF-IDF reveals that the two methods often detect different kinds of similarity inside the same corpus. Stylo groups texts mainly through repeated grammatical habits and narrative rhythm, while TF-IDF groups them through distinctive vocabulary and thematic focus. In some cases, the methods strongly agree. Dick and Norton remain relatively stable across both analyses, suggesting that their works share both stylistic consistency and recurring thematic language. Wells’s *The Salvaging of Civilization* also remains isolated in nearly every visualization, showing that its nonfiction structure and political vocabulary separate it sharply from the surrounding science fiction corpus regardless of method.

The most interesting cases, however, are the disagreements between the methods. Jackie Sees a Star appears distant in both analyses, but for different reasons. Stylo isolates it because its narrative voice and sentence structure differ from the rest of the corpus, while TF-IDF isolates it because its vocabulary diverges from dominant science fiction themes. Brackett and Kuttner create a similar tension. Their texts remain unstable in the stylometric models, yet some of them move closer together thematically under TF-IDF because they share overlapping genre concerns. These disagreements show that style, genre, and content cannot always be separated cleanly. The computational models become most useful not when they produce perfect agreement, but when they expose places where literary form becomes unstable and difficult to classify.

## 5. Reflection
This project showed that computational reading can reveal patterns that are difficult to notice through close reading alone, especially when comparing several authors at the same time. Across the cluster trees and PCA plots, some authors remained consistently grouped together even when the number of most frequent words changed. This suggests that certain stylistic features are relatively stable across texts. At the same time, the analysis also showed that authorial style is never completely fixed. Some works moved between clusters depending on the method or word range used, which suggests that genre, themes, and vocabulary choices can sometimes shape the results as much as authorship itself.

The project also reflects some of the concerns raised by Ted Underwood about distant reading. Underwood argues that computational methods should not be treated as fully objective or separated from interpretation because models still depend on human choices about data, categories, and methods. This became clear in our own analysis. Changing from 100 MFW to 3000 MFW, or shifting from Stylo clustering to TF-IDF PCA, produced different spatial relationships between texts. The methods did not reveal one final truth about the novels. Instead, they highlighted different dimensions of similarity. Some methods emphasized authorial style, while others emphasized thematic or lexical patterns.

Underwood also warns that distant reading can become too focused on technical detail and lose connection with literature itself. Our results show why interpretation still matters. The graphs alone cannot explain why certain texts cluster together or why some novels appear as outliers. Human interpretation is still necessary to connect these visual patterns back to literary style, genre, narrative voice, and historical context. Computational analysis is therefore most useful not as a replacement for literary reading, but as a tool that opens new questions and new ways of seeing relationships between texts.

## Conclusion
This project showed that computational methods can reveal meaningful literary patterns, but they also show how unstable and layered literary style can be. Some authors stayed closely grouped across multiple methods, which suggests that stylistic fingerprints do exist. At the same time, other texts shifted positions depending on the model, reminding us that literary language is shaped by many factors beyond authorship alone. The comparison between Stylo clustering, PCA, and TF-IDF analysis demonstrated that each method captures a different part of textual similarity rather than producing one final or objective answer. More importantly, the project showed that distant reading works best when combined with interpretation instead of replacing it. The graphs helped us see patterns, but interpretation gave those patterns meaning. In the end, computational reading does not reduce literature into numbers. It challenges us to ask a bigger question: if different methods produce different literary maps, then what exactly counts as literary similarity in the first place?
